## The Core Argument:

The core argument is that modern mathematics should be viewed as more of an aesthetic pursuit rather than a pursuit of function, Hardy likened it to that of poetry and sculpture. He also says, much like sculpture and poetry, that ugly mathematics has no place and that all mathematics should be beautiful. 

## What Mathematics is “serious”:

Hardy states that for mathematical work to be serious it needs generality, depth, unexpectedness, inevitability and economy. His examples of work that fits these criteria are Euclid’s proof of infinite primes and the proof of the irrationality of √2. He compares this to chess problems, which he states are merely trivial as they lack true significance to the wider world. He further says that the serious theorems will outlast cultural ideas, with Archimedes outlasting Aeschylus as ‘mathematical ideas do not decay’.

## Dull Maths:

Another distinction made by Hardy is the difference between real and applied maths. He said that while real maths can sometimes be dull and useless, that is its best quality and that it is these reasons that it beautiful. This is compared to applied maths which in his view is ugly as it can cause harm. This was very obvious to Hardy as he watched mathematical concepts be used to cause har during WW2. 

## Why Bother?:

Hardy’s argument is that mathematical work should b continued for intellectual curiosity, pride and ambition. His final justification for the study and pursuit of maths is that it is a harmless occupation that creates beautiful patterns and permanent results.

## My Opinion:

His claim of the uselessness of new mathematics is not at all true. Many modern concepts have slipped right in to function and therefore are far from useless. Further, his view of applied mathematics as dull, while maybe true during his time, is just wrong now. When you look at the mathematical application that has happened to save lives I think this is obvious, especially with the statistical mathematics that was used during the covid pandemic to regulate large populations and control outbreaks. Lastly, its far too melancholic, while mathematics is sometimes dull, it is also brilliant in its unity and how it comes together. This book, to me, seemed like a fed up mathematician who couldn’t spark new ideas anymore frustrated with the subject.
